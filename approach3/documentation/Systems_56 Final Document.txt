Glossary of Definitions
Table 1 - Definitions.
Term
	Definition
	ESP32
	A microcontroller with Wi-Fi and Bluetooth used to control sensors, actuators, and communication in the bridge system.
	Ultrasonic Sensor 
	A distance-measuring sensor used to detect approaching boats.
	Limit Switch
	A mechanical sensor used to detect the fully open or fully closed positions of the bridge span.
	Motor Driver
	An electronic module that allows the ESP32 to control DC motors for lifting and lowering the bridge.
	DC Motor
	A direct current motor used to raise and lower the bridge via the leadscrew mechanism.
	Buck Converter
	A voltage regulation device used to step down from the 12V power supply to lower voltages (5V, 3.3V) for system components.
	Leadscrew Mechanism
	A screw-based mechanical system driven by motors to lift the bridge span smoothly.
	TPM (Technical Performance Measure)
	A measurable indicator used to verify whether the system meets functional and performance requirements.
	FSM (Finite State Machine)
	A computational model that manages the different operational states of the bridge (Closed, Opening, Open, Closing, etc.) and their transitions.


	ROM (Range Of Motion)
	A phrase describing where the mentioned object is able to move to.
	UI (User Interface)
	The system interface through which users can monitor and control the bridge.
	Remote UI
	A web-based dashboard that displays bridge status and allows manual overrides of bridge operations.
	Inclusions
	Tasks and responsibilities that the Systems Group is directly accountable for (e.g., wiring, programming, UI development).
	Exclusions
	Tasks that the Systems Group is not responsible for (e.g., structural design, material procurement).
	Gantt Chart
	A project management tool that represents the project schedule, milestones, and dependencies over time.

1. Introduction


As Engineering students, each team is expected to design, prototype, build and iterate a working Opening Bridge system. Teams are composed of engineers of different disciplines, coming together in order to collaborate and make a fully working artefact. Within the scope of this project, Group Systems 56 is responsible for all movement related components. Our Group is paired with other Systems Groups 52 and 54, as well as our shared Structures 49, who are responsible for designing and building the structure of the bridge. Together, we are Team WM2.  Group selection of parts, connecting of components, building the bridge, and integration with the communications system is critical to the project's success. This document will explore with detail the problem definition, subsystems involved, the requirements of the task and its associated Technical Performance Measures (TPMs), deliverables and inclusions/exclusions of the team. In addition, a detailed overview of the preliminary design, as well as other considered designs are included too.




________________
2. Problem definition
2.1 Problem Statement[a]
A client has requested that we build a sensor-driven and remotely-controllable opening bridge that spans an imaginary river, which will operate at 44WTR Engineering Building. The bridge’s main function is to open and close to allow boats to pass through. In addition, when the bridge is closed, it should allow for pedestrians and vehicles to cross. The opening bridge system is expected to have certain functions in order to meet a list of requirements. These requirements range from bridge design, load capacity and technological features such as an automatic control system for detecting the arrival of boats [1].

5.2 Technical Performance Measurement Breakdown
5.2.1 Electrical/Mechatronic TPMs
Table 10 - TPM.EL01 Sensing Incoming Boats
TPM Code
	TPM.EL01
	Name of TPM
	Sensing Incoming Boats
	Purpose of TPM
	Ensure the sensors detect boats at a reasonable distance from the bridge, and don’t cause false alarms.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.01 (boats must be detected at a certain distance)
	Risk Level (low, medium, high)
	High
	What should be measured
	The distance from the bridge at which the boats are detected by the ultrasonic sensors, and the angle at which the sensor can detect boats.
	How should it be measured
	It should be measured by using an object with the same size to replicate the boat, and move it towards the boat until it is detected by the sensor. This distance will be measured. Another method is to place the object at known distances to the sensor, and check if there are any significant errors in the measured distance by the sensor. In addition, an object can be placed at a known distance, and can be moved at different angles to the sensor to determine the maximum angle range it can be detected.
	How often should it be measured
	It should be mainly tested in the preliminary stages, where just the ultrasonic sensor is tested alone. Once it is integrated into the system, it should be measured a few times to confirm the placement of the sensors allows all boats to be detected.
	Measures of success
	The boat is detected far enough from the bridge that it allows the light states to change, and gives the boat enough distance to stop before getting too close to the bridge.
	Measures of failure
	The sensors either do not detect the boat, or detect it when the boat is too close to the bridge.
	Possible causes of failure
	The positioning of the ultrasonic sensor is incorrect, or the sensor doesn’t work, or the sensor hasn’t been setup correctly.
	





Table 11 - TPM.EL02 Clearing the Bridge
TPM Code
	TPM.EL02
	Name of TPM
	Clearing the Bridge
	Purpose of TPM
	Test to ensure that there is enough time for pedestrians and vehicles to clear from the bridge before it lifts to allow a boat to pass.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.05 (clear bridge of vehicles/pedestrians)
	Risk Level (low, medium, high)
	High
	What should be measured
	Measure the time the clearing state is on for, which allows pedestrians and vehicles to clear the bridge, before the bridge begins to move.
	How should it be measured
	It should be measured by simulating this bridge scenario, and measuring the time of the clearing state, and checking if that is enough time to clear the bridge. Different scenarios should be simulated, such as a very busy bridge.
	How often should it be measured
	It should be measured many times until the selected time is enough to safely clear the bridge.
	Measures of success
	There is enough time for the bridge to be cleared of vehicles and pedestrians, with a few extra seconds to spare.
	Measures of failure
	There is not enough time to clear, resulting in the bridge moving while pedestrians and/or vehicles are on the bridge, which can be very dangerous and fatal.
	Possible causes of failure
	Not enough time was allocated to the clearing state, which is done through the software.
	

























Table 12 - TPM.EL03 Smooth Bridge Movement
TPM Code
	TPM.EL03
	Name of TPM
	Smooth Bridge Movement
	Purpose of TPM
	Test that the bridge raises and lowers at a smooth pace that is reasonable speed.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.09 (bridge must regulate speed)
	Risk Level (low, medium, high)
	Low
	What should be measured
	The speed that the bridge raises and lowers, as well as the time it takes the bridge to move from its completely raised position to its completely lowered position. 
	How should it be measured
	It should be measured by moving the bridge up and down and conducting the measurements while it is moving.
	How often should it be measured
	It should be measured many times until the speed is adequate.
	Measures of success
	The bridge moves at a reasonable speed, that is not too slow or not too fast.
	Measures of failure
	The bridge moves very slowly or too quickly, which can make the system dangerous. 
	Possible causes of failure
	The speed of the motors was set incorrectly (either too fast or too slow).
	



































Table 13 - TPM.EL04 Fitment of Components during Motion
TPM Code
	TPM.EL04
	Name of TPM
	Fitment of Components during Motion
	Purpose of TPM
	Ensure all components are securely mounted within the bridge and stay in place during motion.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.04 (design shall fit all components)

	Low

Table 14 - TPM.EL05 Levelled bridge
TPM Code
	TPM.EL05
	Name of TPM
	Levelled Bridge
	Purpose of TPM
	Ensure that when the bridge is moving, it remains leveled.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.06 (bridge shall remain levelled)
	Risk Level (low, medium, high)
	Medium
	What should be measured
	The angle that the bridge moves at, as well as the angle of the bridge when it stops at its highest and lowest point.
	How should it be measured
	Limit switches will be used to ensure the bridge hits its maximum height on both sides. So confirming that the bridge hits the sensor will be how it is measured.
	How often should it be measured
	It should be measured many times during the assembly of the system, so features such as the component placement and coding can be adjusted to meet the requirement before the final system is completed.
	Measures of success
	Both sides of the bridge hit the limit switch which is set at 200mm.. In addition, the bridge should be leveled with the road when it is at its minimum height.
	Measures of failure
	Only one side of the bridge hits the limit switch, the bridge doesn’t align with the road, the bridge is not completely straight during its motion and when it is stationary.
	Possible causes of failure
	Incorrect setup of motors, flaws with the mechanical design (using 2 motors and a lead screw design).
	



























Table 15 - TPM.EL06 Bridge Maximum Height
TPM Code
	TPM.EL06
	Name of TPM
	Bridge Maximum Height
	Purpose of TPM
	Certify that when the bridge is at maximum height, it proves a minimum of 200mm clearance to ensure all boats are able to fit through while crossing.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.23 (bridge must be high enough to allow all boats to pass)
	Risk Level (low, medium, high)
	Medium
	What should be measured
	The height of the bridge when it is completely raised, confirming the limit switches are placed at 200mm, and ensuring the bridge hits the limit switches on both sides.
	How should it be measured
	It should be measured by raising the bridge, and making these measurements when the bridge is at its maximum height position.
	How often should it be measured
	It would only need to be measured a few times initially as the bridge motion coding is being completed. Once it has been tested and confirmed a few times, it would be safe to assume each time the bridge is raised, it will meet the height requirement.
	Measures of success
	The bridge is raised at 200mm at its maximum, which will ensure all boats can fit under it.
	Measures of failure
	The bridge does not rise to 200mm at its maximum, which could prevent larger boats from fitting under the bridge, causing an accident.
	Possible causes of failure
	The duration of the motors running was set too little, resulting in the motion of the bridge stopping before the required height.
	

















Table 16 - TPM.EL07 Bridge Supporting Weight
TPM Code
	TPM.EL07
	Name of TPM
	Bridge Supporting Weight
	Purpose of TPM
	Ensure that the bridge is able to support at least 3kg of weight when centred on the roadway, which ensures it can carry the pedestrians and vehicles crossing it.
	Source Requirement
	REQ.07 (bridge must hold weight of vehicles and pedestrians)
	Risk Level (low, medium, high)
	High
	What should be measured
	The weight on the bridge should be measured, as well as the stability of the bridge, to ensure at maximum weight capacity the bridge is stable and functions properly.
	How should it be measured

6. Final Design[d]
6.1 Overview        [e]


The bridge has been designed to mimic a vertical lift bridge, it allows traffic and pedestrians to cross it, and allows boats to pass underneath. The design ensures safe, automated operation with near real-time monitoring and manual overrides via remote user interface (UI). 


Systems 56’s design for the Opening Bridge consists of the following subsystems:
* Bridge Lifting Mechanism
* Electrical Power & Connections
* State Indication & Safety
* State Machine
* Remote User Interface




The electrical subsystem is responsible for the hardware design and its integration with software component, while the software subsystem manages logic, communication, and user interaction (manual overrides). 


The overall architecture is modular, aligning with the Design Structure Matrix (DSM) for interactive development and testing. Key software components include a ESP32 microcontroller with Arduino libraries for embedded control, Wi-Fi for real time data transfer, and Node.js server for backend API management. The frontend UI is built with React and Material- UI for modern, responsive experience. 
 
The system uses a finite state machine (FSM) with 10 states operations, ensuring safety traffic through time transition and sensor feedback. The ESP32 handles local control, while the server enables remote monitoring and overrides. Communication via HTTP, with the ESP32 polling the server every 200ms for command and sending state updates.

6.2 Bridge Lifting Mechanism
The Bridge Lifting (and lowering) Mechanism uses a dual leadscrew design to accurately position the bridge. The systems portion of this subsystem consists of the following components:


* 2 x Brushed DC Motors
* 1 x Motor Driver
* 2 x Limit Switches


The bridge lifting mechanism utilises a dual-leadscrew design. Each motor drives a leadscrew in order to lift and lower the platform. The two brushed DC motors are driven with a single dual-channel motor driver, this allows rudimentary control of the motor speeds and rotation directions.


A pair of limit switches are positioned at the bridge’s range of motion (ROM) limits. These switches are activated when the bridge reaches either the top or bottom of its ROM. This feedback allows the system to deactivate the motors when appropriate, preventing possible damage to the machine.


As a torque requirement was never provided to us by the structures team, we weren’t able to select a motor with a specific torque in mind. However, while we did not know the torque requirement, experimental testing was conducted with a motor of similar characteristics and it was determined that the selected motors would be suitable.


The Makerverse 2-channel Motor Driver has been selected to drive the pair of brushed DC motors. Our reasoning for why this motor driver is suitable for our system is as follows:







The driver’s operating voltage range of 3–16 V aligns directly with the requirements of our 12 V motors, and its acceptance of 3.3 V logic signals ensures compatibility with the ESP32 microcontroller used in our system. The default DIR and PWM mode further simplifies integration, allowing speed and direction control via the ESP32’s PWM outputs.


The FSM would activate the motors in states like PENDING_OPEN (lift) and BRIDGE_CLOSING (lower) using `digitalWrite`. The ESP32 uses analogWrite for speed control and digitalWrite for direction. Integrated into FSM for timed operations (e.g. 3s open/close delay). The ESP32 reads the limit switches via a `digitalRead`, halting motors and updating the FSM’s state.


6.3 Electrical Power & Connections
For detailed information on the electrical power and signal connections, refer to the circuit schematic in ‘appendix 12.3’. Our system utilises 12V, 5V & 3.3V electricity, our power regulation subsystem is responsible for providing these to the rest of the system. We have put detailed thought into cables and connectors in order to ensure the system can be integrated reliably and with ease.


The input to the power regulation subsystem is unregulated +12V DC, this is fed into a buck converter in order to produce +5V DC (regulated), and the ESP32 then produces 3.3V logic signals. 


  

Figure 10 - Power Regulation Flowchart


The buck converter provides protection against any AC components in the +12V input signal. The +5V DC supplied by the buck converter is used to power a large number of the components, while the 3.3V logic voltage is typically only used to control other components (e.g to switch a transistor).
















Our reasoning for the suitability of our chosen buck converter is as follows:














The vast majority of the electronics will be assembled onto a single perfboard, this both reduces the clutter of wires that would otherwise be necessary, and it also allows for the electronics to be much more reliable. Some components that may need to be removed in certain cases will be mounted to the perfboard via header pins, this would allow for the replacement of many vital components.


For the components that cannot be mounted to the perfboard itself, they are connected via cables which plug into header pins soldered onto the perfboard.




















6.4 State Indication & Safety
The bridge’s state indication & safety functions are performed by three subsystems: 
* Traffic Lights
* Speaker Alarm
* Boom Gates


The traffic light subsystem is responsible for signalling to cars, pedestrians and boats when it is safe to go, and when they must wait. This is the bridge’s primary system for directing traffic when to go and when not to go. The bridge has 4 sets of traffic lights on it: Two sets are for cars and pedestrians on either side of the bridge, and the other two sets are for the boats in either direction. 


A set of traffic lights is made up of three LEDs (red, yellow and green) and is controlled according to the finite state machine (FSM):


* DEFAULT_STATE: Green A, Red B.
* SHIP_DETECTED: Yellow A, Red B.
* TRAFFIC_CLEAR, PENDING_OPEN, PENDING_CLOSE, BRIDGE_CLOSING: Red A, Red B.
* BRIDGE_OPEN, STOPPING_BOAT: Red A, Yellow B.
* BOAT_PASSING: Red A, Green B.
* TRAFFIC_READY: Yellow A, Red B.


The speaker is used to sound an alarm before the bridge will begin opening, so that any pedestrians already on the bridge are warned and have enough time to exit the bridge. A slide switch will be wired in series with the speaker, so that in the case it disturbs tutors it can be disabled. In the software, the speaker will be controlled using the tone() function, allowing frequency control of the speaker.

The boom gates are set up to block cars and pedestrians from crossing the bridge while it is raised. They are actuated by micro servos, and controlled with a PWM signal from the ESP32.


The finite state machine (FSM) commands the servos to lower/raise in states like TRAFFIC_CLEAR (lower) and TRAFFIC_READY (raise), this is achieved using servo library. 


6.5 State Machine
The FSM is the core software component, implemented on the ESP32 to manage 10 states for safe bridge operation. It uses timers, sensors for ship detection, and server commands for overrides. 


The ultrasonic sensors are responsible for detecting any present ships, this is achieved by monitoring the distance values acquired from the sensors. Once the program detects a change in the measured distance, it will transition from state 0 to state 1 in the finite state machine (FSM). Software reads via `pulseIn` and debounces for reliability. 


* FSM States and Outputs: 
   * State 0 (Default State): Bridge closed, traffic allowed (Green A, Red B); Speaker off; Boom gates open.
   * State 1 (Ship Detected): Clearing traffic (Yellow A, Red B); Speaker beeps; Boom gates closing.
   * State 2 (Traffic Clear): Traffic stopped (Red A, Red B); Speaker off; Boom gates closed.
   * State 3 (Pending to Open): Opening (Red A, Red B); Speaker beeps; Boom gates closed.
   * State 4 (Bridge Open): Boat ready (Red A, Yellow B); Speaker off; Boom gates closed.
   * State 5 (Boat Passing): Boat moving (Red A, Green B); Speaker off; Boom gates closed.
   * State 6 (Stopping Boat): Stop boats (Red A, Yellow B); Speaker beeps; Boom gates closed.
   * State 7 (Pending to Close): Closing prep (Red A, Red B); Speaker off; Boom gates closed.
   * State 8 (Bridge Closing): Closing (Red A, Red B); Speaker beeps; Boom gates closed.
   * State 9 (Traffic Ready): Traffic ready (Yellow A, Red B); Speaker off; Boom gates opening.


Fig.1 The chart of FSM


* System Behavior per State:
   * Speaker: Planned to beep in SHIP_DETECTED and TRAFFIC_CLEAR to alert traffic.
   * Traffic Lights: Controlled as per state table (e.g., Green A in DEFAULT_STATE, Red A in TRAFFIC_CLEAR).
   * Boom Gates: Planned to lower in TRAFFIC_CLEAR and raise in DEFAULT_STATE.
* Implementation: Defined in esp32_client.ino using enum State, with transitions driven by button presses (GPIO 26), timers (e.g., 5s for trafficClearDelay), and server commands (open, close, clear).

6.10 Alternative Designs Considered[g]
6.10.1 Hardware
The current design for the bridge utilises two brushed DC motors to each spin a leadscrew in order to lift and lower the bridge. An alternative design that we have been considering since the start of the semester is to instead, use a single motor. In either case here the bridge would be a vertical lift bridge, but the current design suffers the issue that it is vulnerable to the eventual desynchronization of the motors. As the lead screws need to stay synchronized, this could potentially lead to an issue down the track once the full system has been integrated.


Alternatively, a single motor design would not suffer these synchronization issues. The alternative design we are considering would use the motor to coil up a string, and as this string is coiled/uncoiled the platform will be either pulled up or let down. The diagram below pictures this new system:
  

Fig. 12 The Current And Alternative Design Contrasted




We will aim to pivot our design to this new system in the coming weeks with the ambition of improving our system’s reliability. However, as we have not made the move yet we unfortunately could not write this prototype document based on the new design.
6.10.2 Software
The current design for the bridge control system is IoT-based architecture with an ESP32 microcontroller handling hardware control with a 10-state finite machine for automation. It uses Wifi for communication, a Node.js/Express server for API endpoints and state management, and a React frontend with Material-UI for the remote user interface. This setup separates the frontend (React SPA) from the server, allowing a real time syncing via HTTP request/response, manual overrides, and visual feedback. 


During development, we considered several alternative designs to balance factors like memory constraints on the ESP32,scalability, real-time performance, security, and ease of maintenance. We also made research on these key alternatives, their advantages and disadvantages. These alternatives were evaluated based on the system’s requirements for autonomy, safety, remote control, and resource efficiency. 




Alternative 1: All - in - one ESP32 Web Server (First Approach) 
This is our first naive approach to build the remote system, host the web UI directly on the ESP32 using libraries like ESPAsyncWebServer. The ESP32 also serves HTML/CSS/JS files, handles API requests, and manages the FSM, eliminating the need for an external server. Communication would be direct via WiFi and using ESP32 as access point. 


Advantages: 
* Simplicity and Cost: No additional server hardware or hosting needed, everything run on the ESP32, reducing setup complexity and costs. 
* Low Latency: direct communication between ESP32 and UI minimizes delay in state updates and command. 
* Offline Capability: Work in isolated environments without internet, as the ESP32 is an access point.


Disadvantages:
* Memory Constraints: The ESP32 has limited RAM and flash storage, making it challenging to host a full UI (HTML/CSS/Js) alongside the FSM, Wifi and HTTP server. This memory limit could lead to unexpected crashes or inability to compile UI. 
* Scalability: Difficult to expand with advanced features like authentication without overloading ESP32. UI updates could be slow during high-load FSM operations. 
* Security: create one point failure, micro controller expose directly to the network vulnerability attack with limited support for advanced security. 
* Development: very hard to debug code with ESP32, when changes and new requirements appear. 










Why we Choose the Current Design 
The current design (ESP32 for embed control, Node.js/Express for APi, React/MUI for frontend) has been selected for its balance of efficiency, scalability, modularity, and security. 
* Memory and Performance: separating the UI from the ESP32 avoids memory limit.
* Modularity: FSM on ESP32, API on server, and React frontend will allow independent development and testing (aligned with DSM). 
* Real-Time Sync: HTTP polling with 200ms frequency provides reliable updates without MQTT’s complexity. 
* Cost-Effectiveness: ESP32 is cheap and low power, Node.js is open source, free to use, React/MUI offers modern UI without heavy overhead. 
* Safety and Reliability: FSM ensures timed transitions with logging for debugging. 
This approach meets the system’s goals for automation, remote control, and safety when allowing future enhancement like MQTT live data or JWT security.

In our dual-leadscrew design, the synchronization of the motors is of the utmost importance. As such we needed to test whether the motors would run at the same speed given the same input voltage.


The test setup consisted of the two motors, a motor driver, a breadboard, a power supply and a camera. The idea is to record the two motors in operation for a minute, and then by counting the number of rotations you have a reasonable estimate of the motor’s RPM.

Buck Converter
We are using the LM2596S step-down (buck) converter to regulate the 12 V main supply down to 5 V. This regulated 5 V output is required to power the ESP32 microcontroller, traffic light LEDs, ultrasonic sensors, servo motors, and buzzer. The converter ensures that these components receive a stable supply within their operating voltage limits.
 
We have verified the operation of the buck converter. We used our lab power supply to provide 12 V DC input to the LM2596S module. We adjusted the knob on the converter until the output voltage was set to exactly 5 V. A digital multimeter was used to measure and confirm the output voltage.


The LM2596S successfully steps down the 12 V input to a stable 5 V output, which will be used as the regulated supply for the system components. This ensures the safe operation of the ESP32, LEDs, ultrasonic sensors, servo, and buzzer in the traffic light.
Traffic Light Simulation
In our design, we have used NPN transistors which are connected to the ESP32 output pins to work as a switch for each LED.  When the ESP32 pin will set high, the transistor will allow current flow through the LED, and when it will low the LED will remain off.  We also used 100Ω resistors to each LED to limit the current and protect the components.
To test our circuit, DC sweep and transient simulations were done in LTspice. This showed us how the  LEDs behave under different supply voltages and how they respond to the ESP32 control signal.
  

Figure 6 - Traffic Light Simulation in LTspice


The first simulation measured the current-voltage characteristics of the red, yellow and green LEDs. The curve shows that the red LED draws the most current, because red LEDs have the lowest forward voltage. The red LED's forward voltage is around 1.9-2V where yellow is about 2.1-2.2V and green is 2.5-2.7V. Since we used the same series resistor for each LED, the LED with the lowest forward voltage which is red, allows more current to pass.
For safe operation and long lifetime, the LED current should remain in the range of 12–18 mA. From the simulation, it can be concluded that as long as the supply stays between 4.6 V and 5.4 V, the LEDs remain within this recommended range.


         Figure 7 - Simulated IV curves for red, yellow, and green LEDs under DC sweep
The second simulation was conducted to simulate the ESP32 pin driving the LED circuit through the NPN transistor. A 3.3 V source was used to represent the ESP32 GPIO output. When the input voltage from the pin is below 1 V, which is pin low, the transistor does not conduct, and the LED current is essentially zero, which keeps the LEDs off. Again, when the pin is high, which is above 1V, the transistor flows current and the LED turns on.
This simulation confirmed that by coding the ESP32 to set the pins high or low in sequence, we can control the LEDs to behave like a traffic light.

Traffic Light System / User Interface
                                Figure 9 - Circuit setup simulating traffic light system.




We will be using an ESP32 as a wireless controller for our traffic light demo. When the ESP32 is connected to a direct power supply it will host its own WiFi connection so any device such as a phone or laptop can connect directly to it. After logging in using the admin credentials you will have a simple homepage with big buttons to change the current stage of traffic (for example, prioritising boats or letting road traffic flow). When you press one of these buttons the page will show the current status and the lights will match it on the board. red, yellow/amber, and green lights for the “traffic” side, and matching indicators for the “boats” side. There’s also a physical push-button on the board so you can flip the main state even without the web page, which is handy for demonstrations and safety.


For testing, we stepped through each stage to make sure the UI, the LEDs, and the logic all lined up. We checked that commands from the page were reflected on the lights with minimal delay, that the physical button acted as a reliable override, and that the system recovered cleanly after power cycles or reconnecting a phone.Everything was looking great until we tried to return to a normal state (boats at red and traffic at green). In the specific use case the green traffic light would not come alight, at first we suspected a software bug but after thorough examination we were stumped and unable to find a reason as to why it didn't work, which made us check the wiring and pins.


After some research into the ESP32 pin documentation and how certain boards work we found out that certain pins on certain boards were input only, notably pin 34 and 35 on our specific board. We had our yellow and green LEDs on those pins so we researched alternatives that could output the light and found that pins 18 and 19 work, we updated the wring and pin numbers and started retesting and every single use case scenario worked as intended.
